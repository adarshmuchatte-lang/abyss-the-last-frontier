Expedition log

A short history of going down

1960

The bathyscaphe Trieste carries Jacques Piccard and Don Walsh to the bottom of Challenger Deep, roughly 10,900 m down.

1977

The submersible Alvin helps reveal hydrothermal vent communities near the Galápagos, life powered by chemistry, not sunlight.

1985

A French–American team led by Robert Ballard locates the wreck of the Titanic at about 3,800 m.

2012

James Cameron makes the first solo descent to Challenger Deep in the submersible Deepsea Challenger.

2022

Shackleton’s ship Endurance, lost in 1915, is found in the Weddell Sea at about 3,000 m.

What the deep keeps

Ships beneath the surface

RMS Titanic≈ 3,800 m

Sunk in 1912 in the North Atlantic. Iron-eating bacteria and “rusticles” are slowly consuming the steel.

Endurance≈ 3,008 m

Crushed by sea ice in 1915 and found in 2022. Cold water has left it remarkably well preserved.

USS Samuel B. Roberts≈ 6,895 m

A US Navy destroyer escort sunk in 1944, reported as the deepest shipwreck ever surveyed.

Wrecks as habitatNew reefs

Sunken ships, like whale carcasses and fallen wood, become islands of food and shelter for deep-sea animals.

Hydrothermal vents

Life without sunlight

Where seawater seeps through hot rock, it returns to the seafloor loaded with minerals. At “black smokers” these fluids can reach around 350–400 °C. Bacteria turn chemicals such as hydrogen sulfide into energy by chemosynthesis, feeding tube worms, clams and shrimp.

Elsewhere, food arrives as marine snow: a steady fall of dead plankton and waste from sunlit waters. It can take weeks to sink to the deep seafloor, and it carries carbon out of the atmosphere’s reach.

How we reach it

Instruments of exploration

Crewed submersibles

Alvin, operated by Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, is certified to about 6,500 m and carries three people.

ROVs

Remotely operated vehicles are tethered to a ship by cable, streaming live video and sampling with robotic arms.

AUVs

Autonomous underwater vehicles follow programmed routes without a tether, mapping the seabed with sonar.

Multibeam sonar

Ships time sound pulses bounced off the bottom to map the seafloor. Most depth maps are made this way.
